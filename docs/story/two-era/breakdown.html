Story Breakdown

Seven episodes across two centuries of the same city. Each episode has a home century, so the player always knows which game they are in — and the present never goes dark, because the one party member who can never cross is working it the whole time.

Provisional. This arc was chosen so later design has something to push against — the episode count, Vesper's slot and the position of several beats are explicitly unsettled. The narrative itself lives in The Story; the arguments live in docs/story/two-era/discussions/.

Episode by episode

EPISODE ONE · Present partly built

The Missing Woman

A disgraced enforcer takes a missing-person case and finds a hole in the city that goes the wrong direction — not only deeper, but earlier.

This is the existing Act 1, re-cut. Nothing in it is discarded; the ending changes. Where the old structure closed on four open threads, the episode now closes on a door — Soraya's map and Kos's knowledge converging on a wound whose signature predates its own installation.

  1. Rooftop, 3AM. Six beats before the case exists. A practitioner whose licensed sigil has been draining her in her sleep; a fixer lifting jailbroken sigils; a lobby terminal that pulses once, wrong, and goes dark.
  2. Wandering Star. A recovery job from a Gray with leverage, ending in a Sprawl clinic where Vesper's name is said twice in ten seconds.
  3. She's Lost Control. The abandoned apartment, Margot's document case, the bar fight, and the first descent into the Meridian. Iris joins; the hub opens.
  4. Inside the cable temple. Four zones down to an exposed ley wound. Kos surfaces and vanishes. Vesper is glimpsed, and whatever looks back is aware.
  5. The Great Below. Junction 9, forty years of a neighbourhood sleeping beside a pipeline, Gideon working the same blocks from the other side. Soraya signs the log: I have the map.
  6. The door. New ending beat. The map and the Meridian agree on a place where the wrong geometry is wrong about when.

EPISODE TWO · Era

The Festival of the Night's End

They cross, and arrive in a flooded square where the thing they have been excavating for four cases is printed on a broadsheet and sold for coins.

The first crossing establishes the rules by breaking them gently: who can go, what the between-space feels like, and the fact that nobody here is surprised by magic because nobody here has been lied to about it yet.

The episode's engine is dramatic irony. The city is celebrating. The party knows how it ends.

  1. The crossing. Through the wound. Half the party cannot follow, and the reason is not yet understood by anyone.
  2. The square. THE POWER IS YOURS. IT ALWAYS WAS. Reid reads the sentence the Bureau spent two centuries burying. Karim is loud in a crowd that agrees with him.
  3. The edges. Iris reads the room and finds the Accountants' tables in the corners, taking names. Margot finds the first census.
  4. The sealed quarter. A blessed district nobody will discuss. Inside: a generation still standing around, breathing and blank. The party coins the title.
  5. The collapse. A community coming apart under the new census — and one person staying. Āio founds the school on screen and then hands it to a student.
  6. The criers. The Office of Civic Output is announced mid-festival. The noose threads in real time, and the episode ends on the party realising what they are standing in.

EPISODE THREE · Both centuries

Two Fronts

The party breaks. Half of them are arguing with a committee that is inventing the Bureau; the other half are holding a city that is being eaten faster every week.

The FF6 scenario split, relocated into the episodic frame. The player moves between two teams with different problems, different tools and no way to talk to each other.

It is also the episode where the party's own composition stops being neutral. Somebody notices who can cross and who cannot, and the pattern is an exact inverse of the registry.

  1. Era-side: the Committee chambers. Radicals and moderates both quoting the broadsheet. The Accountants writing both sets of minutes.
  2. Era-side: the draft. A young clerk writes the first consent form for the quieting, as mercy. Margot finds the oldest page in her own case file — signed by a man she has not met.
  3. Present-side: holding. Kos runs the case with whoever stayed. Disappearances accelerate; Kurtz is not slowing down for anyone's archaeology.
  4. Present-side: the map. Soraya surfaces at last — the terminals that predate their own install dates are the only reason the crossing was findable. She joins.
  5. The bridge. What the era-side brings back is unreadable to everyone except one woman in a Sprawl bar. Paz's silence breaks. She knew. She has always known.
  6. The reunion that isn't. The teams reconverge, and the party is now carrying a secret about itself: being metered means being pinned.
UnsettledWhat actually breaks Paz's silence is still open — see discussions/paz.md. The current placeholder is that she is the only one who can read what comes back, which forces her hand rather than letting her choose.

EPISODE FOUR · Era

The Night of the Lamps

Freedom was won. Then freedom produced a horror, and the horror wrote a charter. The party watches both, and can prevent neither.

The era's set-piece and the iron rule's hardest test. Everything the party learns here is evidence; none of it is leverage.

  1. The blank ones move in step. The sealed quarter wakes. Whatever is unspooling out of one person is reaching the rest of their generation.
  2. The cordon. Varenne does everything correctly, against an enemy that occupies people rather than space. It holds for an hour.
  3. The hour he loses. Playable or witnessed — unsettled. His own troops, in a street they were told was safe.
  4. The lamps. Somebody fires the district. Burning rain, a quarter alight under a sky that never lightens, figures walking in step through it.
  5. The survivor. Spent, lucid, no longer dangerous. A few lines, never a monologue. Then Varenne kills them with an unregistered blade.
  6. The mercy. The Committee's answer: the army could only kill it afterward; we will not be reduced to that again. The quieting is drafted as the humane alternative, by a clerk sincerely preventing a murder.

Present-side, in parallel: the disappearance cohort file reaches Gideon's desk as routine paperwork. The episode ends with him reading it.

EPISODE FIVE · Present

The Last Report

A good man is asked to write the clean version of people being consumed, and discovers that the truthful report and the correct report are different documents.

The single-character episode. The party re-forms around someone else's collapse, and the game's quietest horror — a missing field on a form — does more damage than any dungeon.

  1. The file. Calibrated Grade III+ practitioners. Transferred. No subsequent output readings. No assessments filed.
  2. The order. Bring in Margot. The institution asks for both things in the same week.
  3. The attempt. He writes it. Several times. Each version is either untrue or unfileable.
  4. The exit. He stops being Bureau. How is still open — resignation by the book is the current recommendation, surrendering badge and sigil across a desk.
  5. The door. It works for him. Nobody told him it would. The leash is named out loud.
  6. Kurtz. With the party exposed and a Gold Coat no longer able to pretend they are archaeologists, the present stops being safe ground.
UnsettledHow his metering ends — resign, jailbreak, or be stripped — is TBD. It changes what he carries into the endgame: a man with no sigil at all fights unamplified for the rest of the game.

EPISODE SIX · Era

The Signing

The revolution ends with a quorum, not a battle — and the party is in the room with the paperwork.

The era's last visit and its coldest. Everything that has been promised structurally gets paid here: the acronym, the coat, the clause, the man who wrote the walls, and the woman the whole case was about.

  1. The engrossed copy. Thibault finds two sentences he did not write, in a hand he does not recognise. He wrote the walls; someone else wrote the lock.
  2. The objection. He fights it by the rules — files, requests review, appeals to a process that was never going to answer. The player already knows he loses.
  3. The footnote. Neural Output eXchange is proposed as neutral technical language and passes without comment. The great reveal, watched as administrative tidiness.
  4. The one conversation. They reach Vesper. It is genuinely her, briefly, and she talks about the century they are standing in rather than about herself. Then she is gone.
  5. The quieting of the author. Thibault refuses to sign. The first calibration in the city's history is performed on the man who drafted the document authorising it. He is polite afterward. He goes back to his desk.
  6. The coat. Gold, fitted, saluted. The receipt issued. The party goes home with the broadside, the first census and the draft consent form — the oldest pages of the case.

Somewhere in here, a door the party has used before is attended when they arrive. Nothing is being counted. It notices when it notices.

EPISODE SEVEN · Present

The Status Quo Ante

Not a victory. The best available outcome is putting something back exactly as it was before anyone settled here.

The city is the dungeon. The party has two centuries of evidence, a Gold Coat accelerating toward a manifestation he does not understand, and an institution that cannot be destroyed because it is not a conspiracy — it is infrastructure.

  1. The case, filed. What the era produced is evidence, and evidence has to be used on somebody. Who the party shows it to is the episode's first real choice.
  2. Kurtz. The same speech Varenne gave, by a man who does not know he is an echo — and a party that has already watched how it ends.
  3. WARDEN. Not a confrontation so much as a discovery: there is no room to storm, no face to beat. The handwriting is the whole organisation.
  4. Vesper. Unsettled. Recovered rather than rescued, present-side, late — or else she is not playable at all and the roster stands at nine.
  5. The Ruins. Down to the crash site. Where it has been stuck the whole time, and where the city has spent its existence working a splint loose.
  6. The stranding. Stop the crossing short. Nothing is destroyed, nothing is avenged. What people get back is only what was always theirs — which is the point, and which is harder.
