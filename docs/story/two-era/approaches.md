# The Revolution Era — Three Approaches

> **Status:** Discussion draft from the brainstorming session. Nothing in this document is
> canon, decided, or in the game. Generated 2026-11-05. Working names are flagged
> `[working name]`. Nothing changes until one approach is chosen, designed in detail, and
> approved — and even then, the era would be added to the story docs deliberately, not by
> accident.

The question on the table: **FF6 and Chrono Trigger become the game's two greatest
influences, and the game gains a new timeline the party can travel to — a
Napoleon/French Revolution era.** Decided so far in discussion:

- The era is a **past era of NOX itself** — same city, different century (CT style).
- The era is **one era, revisitable** — a place the party returns to, not a one-off chapter.
- The era is anchored at **the revolution proper** — the founding undecided, the Bureau not
  yet inevitable.
- Selected influence priorities: **FF6** — Kefka-style villain, magic-as-harvest truth,
  theatrical set-pieces, bittersweet non-triumphal ending. **CT** — the entity as Lavos,
  eras as living history, the past resisting change, era set-pieces. (Explicitly *not*
  selected: FF6's World-of-Ruin restructure, CT's personal-arcs-resolved-across-time.)

---

## Shared Foundation (applies to all three approaches)

### How the era is reached

The Meridian's wrong geometry, at its deepest, bends wrong *time*. The party finds a
threshold — a night that isn't tonight. This extends the existing wrong-geometry theme by
one step, and it converges three threads the game already has:

- **Lain's anomaly** — her unregistered output already pulls the Meridian's wrong geometry
  worse every week. That pull, at the wound the party disrupts in Case 3, is the engine.
- **Soraya's map** — the "forty-seven terminals" thread. The pattern she mapped isn't just
  spatial; some terminals' signatures *predate their own installation dates*. The map points
  not only deeper but earlier.
- **Kos's knowledge** — he knows the deep Meridian better than anyone alive; he's the one
  who can say where the walls go wrong "in a specific way" that isn't geometry.

**When:** the era unlocks after Case 4 — once both Soraya's map and Kos's knowledge exist —
and revisits thread through Act 3. It is the endgame's other half, not a side quest.

### The Iron Rule (all three approaches)

**You cannot change what happened.** The Bureau exists; the charter was signed; the coat
was taken. What you *can* do: witness, document, and return with knowledge and evidence
that changes what the present **means** — CT's "history abhors a paradox" as the era's iron
law. This is also the noir thesis: the past is a crime you can only read, never prevent.

### The era's atmosphere

Permanent night, permanent rain. The entity's night is older than the revolution —
barricades by lamplight, broadsheets pasted wet to stone, floodlit committee halls, no sun,
ever, even here. The city is younger, smaller, and already doomed in a way the present
has forgotten it ever noticed.

### The era's world state `[invented — flagged]`

- **The old regime:** before the Bureau, NOX was administered by a temple-mercantile order,
  the **Patrimony** `[working name]`, which openly tithed psychic output to "the Deep
  Patron" as religion. The tithe was the harvest, unhidden — the meter before the meter.
- **The revolution's thesis:** *"The power is yours. It always was. The tithe was theft."*
  Printed on broadsheets. Said in public. The exact truth the present-day game treats as a
  buried revelation was once common knowledge — that's the era's central horror.
- **The factions:** the **Radicals** (abolish all metering — power belongs to the people),
  the **Moderates** (license and protect — "shield the people from what they carry"), and
  the **Accountants** `[working name]` — the old tithe clerks who survived by changing
  coats, and who write everyone's drafts. They are WARDEN's ancestors. Nobody notices them
  because they were always the ones who wrote things down.
- **The General** `[working name]` — the revolution's best soldier, the Napoleon figure. A
  non-practitioner (a Fighter, in present-day terms) — he never needed a Grade. Beloved,
  undefeated, being courted by the Accountants.
- **No Grid, no Bureau sigils yet:** the era predates both. The entity's information
  register is *nearer the surface* without the Grid to obscure it — the wrong geometry is
  louder, rawer. The tithe works through temple reliquaries `[working name]` — bulkier,
  cruder than modern sigils. The revolution is the moment magic stopped being a fact and
  became a property: the privatizing moment, watched.
- **Era distance from present:** TBD (order of centuries). Deliberately unpinned.

### What every approach explains

The charter's acronym — *Neural Output eXchange*, the present's Kojima-tier reveal — was
decided in public, in this era. The Gold Coat's origin. The first expedition's aftermath.
The origin of the Grade census. All three approaches explain these; they differ in
emphasis.

## Approach 1 — The Truth Was Once Public

**Core fantasy:** walk through history. The era is a living district of revolutionary NOX —
streets, committees, crowds — reachable at will. The horror: the truth the present buries
was once public, said aloud, printed — and you watch it get buried.

**Themes:** the harvest-truth made visible (FF6: the tithe → the revolution → the metering
— same machine, three managers); the entity as Lavos (CT: it survives every regime because
every regime ends up administering it); the banality of co-option (Bureau lens: the lie is
built out of true words); the thesis embodied — "they just convinced you to give it away,"
and you watch the convincing.

### Complete story (four visits, gated by present-day progress)

**Visit 1 — The Festival of the Night's End** `[working name]` *(first crossing)*
Arrival in a flooded square during the celebration of the tithe's abolition. Broadside:
"THE POWER IS YOURS. IT ALWAYS WAS." Reid reads the sentence the Bureau spent two centuries
burying. Karim is loud in a crowd that agrees with him. Margot finds the first census —
practitioners, listed, numbered. Iris watches the crowd with the fixer's eye and finds the
Accountants' tables in the corners, taking names. **Set-piece:** the festival — then the
criers announce the founding of the **Office of Civic Output** `[working name]`, the
Bureau's first name, and the party feels the noose threading in real time. The visit ends
with the era's date: the revolution is six months from its end, and no one in the square
knows it.

**Visit 2 — The Committee Chambers**
The party gets inside as petitioners/observers. The debate between radicals and moderates,
with the Accountants writing *both* sides' minutes. The horror: both factions quote the
broadsheet — the lie is being built out of true words. A young clerk drafts the first
"consent form" for **the quieting** `[working name]` — early calibration, performed as
mercy. Character beats: Karim argues with the clerk about what medicine is for, and loses
on procedure. Margot finds the draft — her document case's oldest page. Reid recognizes
Bureau blue in the banners — the color predates the institution. Iris reads the room's
exits and finds exactly as many as the Accountants want.

**Visit 3 — The Terror**
The radicals are purged as counter-revolutionary. The first quietings are performed — on
the revolution's own radicals — by people who believe it's mercy. Kos's wound, invented,
watched. The General commands the enforcement. **Set-piece:** the public tribunal — a
radical practitioner speaks the thesis aloud one last time, to silence. The party knows the
words; the present calls them a conspiracy theory. The General gives the first half of the
villain's speech as conviction, not corruption: *"I've seen what the tithe fed. It doesn't
care what we call it. Sign the paper."*

**Visit 4 — The Signing (Thermidor)**
The moderates win. The charter is drafted. The acronym — *Neural Output eXchange* — is
proposed as neutral technical language and passes without objection: the present's great
reveal, watched as a footnote at a quorum. The General takes the gold coat — the first —
and salutes. The revolution is over; the Bureau begins. The party returns to the present
with the broadside, the first census, the draft consent form — the oldest pages of the
case.

### Important moments

1. The broadside, read in silence by a man who wore the uniform (Reid, Visit 1).
2. The criers announcing the Office of Civic Output mid-festival — the noose threaded
   (Visit 1).
3. The quieting, performed as mercy, on a true believer (Visit 3).
4. The tribunal speech — the thesis said aloud one last time, to silence (Visit 3).
5. The acronym passing as a footnote (Visit 4).
6. The first gold coat, fitted, saluted (Visit 4).

### Character moments

- **Reid:** the man who wore the uniform reads the founding document. Twenty years of
  Bureau service reframed as the coat's secondhand wear. The disgrace reframes: the night
  he let Vesper walk was the second time in history someone chose wrong against the same
  machine — and the first time, it was a committee vote, not a man.
- **Karim:** treats a revolutionary wounded at the barricades; argues the clerk to a
  standstill and loses on procedure. The era is the one place his verbosity is *normal* —
  and he hates how that feels. *"Here, everyone talks like me. It doesn't help."*
- **Margot:** the case opens on the first census — the numbering began at the founding. Her
  wound ("I filed the form correctly") finds its signature: the first consent form, in the
  handwriting of someone who also believed. Her ideology meets its own origin.
- **Iris:** the revolution's dream walking — never registered, full output, no Grade. The
  era's crowd would have called her a miracle; the Accountants would have called her a
  priority. She reads the exits. The fixer realizes she's been running all her life from an
  institution that was a committee vote — small enough, once, to have been stopped.
- **Kos** *(guest, optional):* the calibrated man meets the first calibration. *"It was
  mercy then too."* His wound invented in front of him.
- **The General:** five beats — hero, offer, purge, speech, coat. The villain's origin as
  tragedy; Kurtz in the present is the farce.
- **Gideon** *(late-game, optional):* the Bureau blue banners at the founding, before the
  Bureau. The institution he serves was a vote. The last report he can't file is born here.
- **Paz** *(late-game, optional):* the revolutionary songs — music as un-metered output,
  the channel the Bureau never thought to meter. The first banned song.
- **Vesper** *(optional, one glimpse):* at the festival's edge, in the crowd — present
  across time, her absence made cosmic. Keep it a glimpse, or nothing — she stays absent.

### Mysteries explained

Charter acronym · Gold Coat origin · WARDEN's survival (the Accountants) · origin of the
Grade census · why the Bureau's lie is built out of true words.

### Risks / weaknesses

- **Largest world-building:** factions, era cast, era music, two centuries of texture. The
  most writing.
- Era-cast affection may dilute the core four's focus.
- The era could outshine the present — the past as the more interesting half of the game.

### Scope

A full district: 4–5 zones, festival/tribunal/signing set-pieces, an era cast of ~8–10
named figures, era-appropriate encounters (regime loyalists, committee guard, the first
Gray-equivalents), the era's own songs.

## Approach 2 — The First Gold Coat

**Core fantasy:** watch the villain be made. A tight, five-beat tragedy about one man — the
General — set against the same revolution. The city is heard, not lived: barricades beyond
the walls, drums in the rain.

**Themes:** the Kefka-villain priority — the speech given twice, once as tragedy (the era),
once as farce (Kurtz, present); the leash (WARDEN); the concept doc's own line, embodied —
*the villain is kind of right, and it doesn't matter anymore because they've seen what's
down there*; hero-to-emperor as the revolution's own arc (Napoleon); the entity as Lavos in
the Ruins glimpse.

### Complete story (five beats, one man)

**Beat 1 — The Hero**
The General's victory address in a flooded square — the same cadences as the present's
villain speeches, delivered unironically, before the fall. Reid recognizes the rhetoric:
Bureau catechism run backward — the original, honest version. The party moves through the
crowd disguised as camp followers.

**Beat 2 — The Descent**
The Accountants invite the General to see what the tithe fed. The party follows — into the
edge of the Ruins, the first-contact site. The entity is visible in the era, as it always
is to those who look. He looks. It looks back. *(The Summoner's line: looked at something
and it looked back.)* The party witnesses the moment that makes every later villain.

**Beat 3 — The Offer**
The Accountants explain the terms. The city runs on output; the revolution will tear it
apart; be the one who holds it together. The Bureau's draft is presented as mercy. The
horror: the offer is WARDEN's, the leash is the point, and the entity itself never even
notices him. The General understands exactly what he's accepting — that's what makes it a
tragedy, not a seduction.

**Beat 4 — The Speech** *(the era's set-piece, its opera scene)*
The villain's speech, delivered to the Committee: *the people must be licensed for their
own protection; the tithe must return as a fee; the truth must be filed away until they're
ready.* The origin of every Bureau catechism, given once as conviction. The party watches
the founding lie spoken into existence by a man who knows it's a lie and believes it
anyway — because he's seen what's down there, and he's not wrong that someone has to
administer the crossing.

**Beat 5 — The Coat**
Fitted, gold, in the corridor after the vote. He salutes. The receipt. The party returns to
the present: Kurtz wears the same coat, gives the same speech, doesn't know he's an echo.
Kurtz is demystified and made scarier at once — he's not original; the room is. And the
party now knows what Kurtz is being offered: the leash — and that accepting it isn't power.
The present-day final confrontation arrives pre-armed: *you've seen how this speech ends.
You've seen the man who took it.*

### Important moments

1. The victory address — rhetoric recognized (Beat 1).
2. The Descent — he looks, it looks back (Beat 2).
3. The Offer — the leash explained as mercy (Beat 3).
4. The Speech — the catechism, in its honest original (Beat 4).
5. The Coat — the receipt, issued and saluted (Beat 5).

### Character moments

- **Reid:** the General is the man Reid might have been — the enforcer who believed, same
  uniform, twenty years. The horror of recognizing his own catechism in its original,
  honest form.
- **Margot:** the first calibration records carry the General's signature — her document
  case's oldest page. Her wound ("I filed the form correctly") meets its author. *"Someone
  signed first."*
- **Karim:** treats the General's wounds after the Descent; argues with him; fails to move
  him. Karim losing an argument is rare — let it land. He talks the man through the choice
  and watches him take the leash anyway.
- **Iris:** reads the Offer as a protection racket — *"the oldest contract in the city, and
  they put it on a coat."* No exits in the room where the offer is made: the fixer without
  a back door.
- **The General:** the era's heart — five beats, one man.
- **Kos** *(optional):* the calibration that made him, authorized at the desk the party is
  standing beside — centuries early.
- **Kurtz** *(present):* demystified by the echo; the present confrontation gets the era as
  its prelude.
- **Gideon** *(late-game, optional):* the speech that founded his institution, delivered
  honestly, before the institution existed — the faith's origin, watched.

### Mysteries explained

Gold Coat origin and meaning (the receipt) · why Kurtz could find WARDEN at all (the trail
is bait — WARDEN wants assets who read the archive) · the Bureau's founding speech ·
the first contact's aftermath.

### Risks / weaknesses

- The city is backdrop — under-serves the revolution texture.
- A single-character era risks becoming a cutscene corridor; the Descent and the Speech
  must be real, playable beats to earn it.
- Thin reasons to revisit beyond the five beats; the era cast is minimal.

### Scope

An encampment, the Ruins gate, the committee chamber — 2–3 zones; five beats; 2–3 named
era figures. Medium build.

## Approach 3 — The Drafting Room

**Core fantasy:** document a crime you can't prevent. One building, one night — the
charter's drafting and signing — entered and re-entered through a door that opens onto
wrong time. The framing is **investigation, not tourism**: Reid is a detective; the signing
is a crime scene; the tension isn't "can we stop it" but *"can we read it in time."* Each
visit holds different doors open — same night, growing access, like re-walking a scene.

**Themes:** the banality of the crime (Bureau lens: the founding was a quorum, not a coup);
the past resists change while evidence persists (CT); the entity editing history — Lavos at
its most intimate; *"the ancient evil has paperwork"* made literal; investigation as time
travel.

### World state

The revolution is *outside* — barricades, drums, rain, heard through the windows. Inside:
the signatories — moderates and Accountants — and the General in the corridor, waiting to
enforce a document he didn't write.

### Complete story (five visits — the night is fixed; access grows)

**Visit 1 — The Archive**
First draft: *"the power is human; it belongs to the people."* Final draft beside it:
*"output is a licensed resource."* The entire thesis of the game as a redline edit on one
page. Margot's case fills. The crime takes shape: not a signature — an erasure.

**Visit 2 — The Committee Chamber**
After the vote. Ledgers, marginalia: the *Aurum* designation, the first Grade table, the
acronym *Neural Output eXchange* filed as "neutral technical terminology." The present's
great reveal, watched as a footnote.

**Visit 3 — The Corridor**
The General, waiting — the era's one living exchange. The room's wrongness makes it
half-dream: *"Who are you? — You have the look of people who read the fine print. Read it.
I've seen what's down there. It doesn't care what we call it. Sign the paper."* The first
gold coat is fitted in the next room. The receipt issued.

**Visit 4 — The Balcony** *(the set-piece)*
The signing, from the margins, above the crowd. The city below celebrates the charter that
buries its own thesis. Rain, lanterns, the crowd cheering the leash — the loudest thing the
party has ever heard, and it's for the wrong thing. The acronym goes up.

**Visit 5 — The Edits**
The documents have changed. The draft the party read in Visit 1 says something else now.
The entity is in the walls — *still editing*. The founding lie isn't historical; it's
maintained, corrected, in real time, from beneath. The horror extends to the present:
Margot's case file is no longer trustworthy — the documents the party has been carrying can
change too. Knowledge as the curse, done to the paperwork.

### Important moments

1. The two drafts side by side — the thesis as a redline edit (Visit 1).
2. The acronym, filed as a footnote (Visit 2).
3. The corridor exchange — the era's only living conversation (Visit 3).
4. The balcony — the crowd cheering the leash (Visit 4).
5. The changed document — the entity's handwriting in the margin (Visit 5).

### Character moments

- **Reid:** the detective at the scene of the oldest crime in the city. The case that
  started with Vesper reaches a table he can't touch — and he keeps taking notes.
- **Margot:** the document case opens fully — the room is where her numbers were born. Then
  Visit 5: the documents change, and her case — her faith, her method, her wound's
  architecture — becomes unstable. The woman who filed the form correctly learns the form
  edits itself.
- **Iris:** no exits — the one room she can't read, because the room isn't there. The
  runner, stopped, in a place that doesn't exist.
- **Karim:** reads the first draft aloud, too loud, in the archive where it was killed. The
  verbosity as eulogy.
- **Kos** *(guest, optional):* the first calibration form is on the table — the consent
  form for the procedure that made him. *"There was a form."*
- **The General:** minimal — the corridor exchange only. Not a tragedy; an instrument. The
  Napoleon figure as a man waiting to enforce a document he didn't write.
- **Gideon** *(late-game, optional):* the room where his institution was voted into
  existence — the faith's paperwork.

### Mysteries explained

Charter acronym · Aurum designation · first Grade table · why the founding documents were
"lost" (they weren't — they're maintained) · the lie's exact moment.

### Risks / weaknesses

- No lived revolution — by design; the user must want the *paperwork*, not the barricades.
- Five visits to one building need the growing-access structure to stay fresh.
- The era cast is one conversation deep — weakest "living history" of the three.

### Scope

One building, five rooms, five visits, one living exchange, one set-piece. Lowest build of
the three — and the strongest as a *first slice*: the room can later widen into Approach
1's district if the game ever wants more.

## Recommendation

**Approach 1 — The Truth Was Once Public** — as the full vision. It is the only one that
delivers every selected priority at full strength: the harvest-truth across three regimes
(FF6), the entity as Lavos surviving every administration of it (CT), three era set-pieces
(FF6), and a past that explains the present but cannot be fixed (CT + noir). Its cost is
real — the most new world-building — but it is the *right* work: the Bureau's missing
origin story, slotted into existing seams rather than bolted on.

**Pragmatic path if scope is the fear:** build **Approach 3 — The Drafting Room** first as
the era's opening beat, with the door able to widen later into Approach 1's district. The
two are compatible: the room is where the lived district's story is decided.

**Choose Approach 2 only if** the Kefka-villain line is the absolute priority and the
revolution's texture matters less than the man. It is the tightest tragedy and the
strongest demystification of Kurtz — but it spends the era on one figure.
