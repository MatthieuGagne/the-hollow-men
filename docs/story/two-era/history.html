The HollowMen

In a city that has never had a morning, magic is licensed, graded, taxed — and quietly taken.

Scroll ↓

A disgraced enforcer takes a missing-person case. The missing woman turns out not to have been taken.

She went — deliberately, because she found something and couldn't look away. Finding out where leads through a bureaucracy that harvests human minds, a city built around a wound, and a century that already knew everything this one has forgotten.

Work in progress

This is a living design document for an unfinished game, not a finished story. The present-day material is settled; the revolution era and everything before it are active drafts, argued out session by session and still changing. Names marked as working names will change. Several decisions on this page contradict each other on purpose, because they have not been reconciled yet — see Canon Status for what is settled, what is draft, and what is still open, and the Story Breakdown for the episode structure.

CHAPTER I

The City That Has No Morning

NOX — Latin for night. The dark is not weather. It is an arrangement.

Nobody in NOX has seen a sunrise, and nobody finds that strange, because there has never been one to miss. The rain is constant. The neon is permanent. The thing beneath the city finds the night useful, and the city has no morning to compare it to.

It is a working metropolis: megacorp towers, licensed practitioners, Bureau enforcers in pressed uniforms, ley terminals lining the boulevards like metro stations. Everything functions exactly as intended. That is the horror. In the Heights there is no visible wrongness — no broken geometry, no rain falling sideways. There is only the absence of friction, of dirt, of the human mess that lives everywhere else, and people content in a way that looks like something was quietly removed.

The city is built in a vertical stack, and the deeper you go, the more honest it gets.

Three layers of people run it, and only one of them knows what it is for.

The corporate war in the Sprawl is real, violent, and deliberately arranged — a conflict that keeps the city divided and looking sideways. WARDEN is not a conspiracy; conspiracies can be exposed. WARDEN is infrastructure.

CHAPTER II

The Thing That Got Stuck

It is not evil. It is not even hostile. It is immobilised.

Long before there were people here, something attempted to cross between registers — from the space between things into physical presence — and it failed. Caught partway, unable to finish and unable to withdraw, it went inert. Not asleep by choice. The way a body in shock goes still.

Everything the city believes about the thing beneath it assumes intent. It has none. A being that could cross freely would need no city, no charter, no Bureau and no Grade system. The entire two-century machine exists because the thing at the centre of it cannot move. Patience reads as strategy. It is a splint.

What leaked out of something stuck halfway through was never a lure. It was a wound bleeding into the local register — and human minds are precisely the instrument that registers that bleed. Nobody heard a call. People felt a place that felt significant, and significance is enough; settlements form around the uncanny all the time.

It drew settlement the way a magnet draws filings.
No wanting involved.

And then there were enough minds, close enough, for long enough — and a stranded thing with one remaining input began, dimly, to register them. Not malevolence. Stimulus.

Nobody trespassed. Nobody touched it. Nobody read a forbidden word or struck a bargain. The crime was demographic: we were here, in numbers, paying attention. Gathering and looking is not a sin anyone can be warned against.

Then an expedition went down into what would later be called the Ruins, and someone died — a fall, a collapse, the ordinary way people die underground. And the thing took what spilled.

The survivors watched a death become an input. No bargain was struck, no relic taken, no reply given. They observed a mechanism, and came back up holding the one fact nobody else in the world had:

It takes this,
and this is how it arrives.

Their inheritors became WARDEN. What they inherited is not a relic and not a secret — it is a method, and that is the hard part. You can burn a document and bury an object. You cannot un-know what something eats.

Two centuries of harvest infrastructure are the industrialisation of an accident witnessed once.

Three things that deliberately never happen rules of the horror
  • The entity never speaks. Not at first contact, not ever. No reply, no bargain, no contract. Every "relationship" the city believes it has is a human invention laid over a mechanism.
  • No artifact comes up from first contact. The one object that travels through the whole story — an unregistered blade — belongs to a much later century.
  • The first victim has no name. No ceremony, no martyr, no monument. Nobody down there died for anything.
CHAPTER III

The Religion of the Tithe

How a mechanism became a faith — and why the faith had evidence.

People kept dying near the site, and the deaths got read as a debt. Giving became prevention. Nobody could disprove it, because the city kept standing. The founders of what became the Patrimony were not cynics and not fanatics. They were insurers.

And then something actually got better. The site quieted. The night became liveable. The settlement grew where it had no business growing. The appeasement acquired evidence, and gratitude layered itself on top of fear.

So for centuries, NOX was run by a temple-mercantile order that did not hide the harvest — it preached it. Psychic output was tithed to the Deep Patron as public religion. You gave because giving was holy, and because the Patron's appetite was understood to be the price of the city existing at all. Collection ran through shrine reliquaries: bulky, crude ancestors of the modern sigil.

Their extraction was no gentler than what replaced it. It was simply honest about existing. Everyone knew they were giving something away. The argument was about whether they should — never about whether they were.

Entirely sincere. Entirely correct about the mechanism.
Wrong about every reason.
CHAPTER IV

The Crime

A bad year, a raised quota, and the cheapest yield available.

Quotas rise. They always do. And in one bad year the order began tithing children, because untrained output is the cheapest thing to take — it doesn't resist, it doesn't notice, and a child has no professional life to disrupt. It was not malice. It was actuarial.

They piloted it in one district, the way any policy is piloted before it is extended. And one generation of that quarter grew up blank: breathing, functional, answering questions correctly, and gone.

The crime is not the harvest. The crime is what came next. The order did not admit an error — it declared the district blessed. The children who gave most were nearest the Patron. Sanctification was how the quarter got quarantined, and quarantine was how it got forgotten.

Admission was never available to them. To concede that the tithe had a limit was to concede that it was not holy. They were sincere, and that is exactly why they could not be honest.

Tolerance figures, known. Quota raised anyway. For years. The word children appears nowhere in any of it.

The clerks wrote "untrained yield" — ancestors of the clerks who would draft consent forms two centuries later, with the house style already in place.

The quarter is still there, sealed, when the party walks into it. It is the one place in that century where what lives under the city is visible without descending to the Ruins. And standing in it, somebody finally says the words that give this game its title — two hundred years before the game they are playing.

CHAPTER V

The Truth, In Public

The revelation this story spends its whole length excavating was once printed on paper and sold in the street.

The revolution overthrew the Patrimony on a single sentence, and the sentence was correct.

THE POWER IS YOURS.
IT ALWAYS WAS.
THE TITHE WAS THEFT.

This is the heart of the whole design. The thing the present-day city treats as a conspiracy theory — that magic is human, internal, and always was yours — was not secret knowledge. It was common knowledge. It was shouted in a flooded square by people who were right, during a festival celebrating the abolition of the tithe.

And then it was buried. Not by a coup, not by a monster — in public, by committee, over about six months, by people quoting that same broadsheet back at each other. The lie that runs the present day was built entirely out of true words, and the party is there to watch it happen.

For a few weeks, the city was the freest it has ever been, or will ever be again.

CHAPTER VI

The Night of the Lamps

Freedom was won. Then freedom produced a horror. Then the horror wrote a charter.

There was a survivor of the child tithe. Not one of the blanked — the one it failed on: partially drained as a child, never trained, carrying output nobody metered and nobody taught.

After abolition there was no Patrimony left to contain them and no Bureau yet to license them. The revolution's victory removed the only thing that had been holding them. And they came apart.

1

The quarter wakes up

They don't attack. They come apart, and the coming-apart has a radius. It begins in the sanctified quarter, where the rest of their generation is still standing around breathing and blank. The city's first sign is that the blank ones have begun moving in step.

2

The General does the correct thing

Cordon, evacuate outward, contain inward. Textbook — and built for an enemy that occupies space, where this one occupies people. The cordon holds about an hour. Then it is inside the cordon, because it was always inside the cordon: it came in on the men he posted there.

3

The hour he loses

He is not stupid and not slow. He is out of category. Discipline, position, numbers, nerve — every instrument he owns is the wrong instrument, and he spends an hour learning that at the cost of the people who trusted him most. A man who has never lost has no framework for this was not losable.

4

The lamps

Permanent night; the quarter runs on oil. Somebody — not him — fires the district to break the spread. It half-works. Burning rain, a quarter alight under a sky that never lightens, and figures walking in step through it, unbothered, because nobody is home to mind the fire.

5

He finds them, and it is already over

Spent. A young adult sitting in the wet, lucid — probably for the first time in the whole event — and aware of what has happened. The only person in the city who both caused this and understands it. And no longer dangerous. Nothing is about to happen.

6

He kills them anyway

With a blade. Unregistered, no serial — the one that ends up in an archive, and then on a very different man's hip, two centuries later.

He gives a different reason at different points for the rest of his life: that a thing which did this cannot be allowed to exist; that they asked him to; that he had just watched his own men die for nothing and needed the night to have an ending.

The fourth reason he never says aloud. A living survivor means testimony, and testimony means the city learns the Patrimony made this, and the city is two days from tearing itself apart. The party can prove that one from the minutes.

He did everything right, and it did nothing,
and then he killed a victim in the rain and called it four different things.
Where Output Calibration actually comes from the Bureau's worst instrument

The Committee's argument afterward writes itself: the army could not stop it; the army could only kill it afterward; we will not be reduced to that again.

So the procedure — a bonded sigil that quietly routes a practitioner's output away from them forever — is drafted as a humane alternative to what the General did. Invented so that nobody would ever again have to put a blade in a victim. The clerk who writes it is sincerely preventing a murder.

That is why, two centuries later, it still has a consent form, a clinical pathway, a forty-minute schedule and a follow-up appointment. The cruellest thing the Bureau does was designed by people reacting in horror at violence.

CHAPTER VII

The Signing

The revolution ends with a quorum, not a battle.

The moderates win. A charter is drafted. The city's new administrative body is named in language so neutral it passes without objection, and the city itself is given an official designation that has been an accurate description of its function ever since.

NOX
Neural Output eXchange

They did not hide what the city was. They named it correctly, in public, and trusted that nobody would read the charter. For two centuries, nobody has.

Three things are born in the same room: the office that becomes the License Bureau, the first census of practitioners, and the procedure drafted to prevent another Night of the Lamps.

And at the signing, a gold coat is fitted and saluted — the first. The General takes it as a promotion. It is a receipt: the designation an older, quieter organisation uses to mark assets it intends to use until there is nothing left to use.

The tithe, the revolution and the metering are the same machine under three managers.

CHAPTER VIII

Two Hundred Years of Forgetting

Nothing was refuted. It was reclassified.

The office becomes the License Bureau. Reliquaries shrink into sigils that slot into flesh and hardware. The census becomes a Grade system, I through V, measuring how efficiently your output interfaces with the collection grid — sold to you as a measure of your talent. The procedure gets a diagnostic code.

The Bureau funds Academies that are, genuinely, excellent schools. The teachers believe what they teach. Nobody in the pipeline is lying, and that is worse than lying. A licensed practitioner who came up through the Academy doesn't experience the Bureau as constraint. It's identity.

And the broadsheet sentence was never argued down. It simply moved: from common knowledge, to radical slogan, to crank theory, to nothing. By the present day a man can say the power was always yours out loud in a bar and be ignored as a drunk.

Output Calibration: a deep-bonded sigil anchored below the layer a practitioner can reach, routing substantially all of their output away and leaving just enough to pass as a person. Officially irreversible. The calibrated describe it the same way, often enough that the description travels as a warning: "Like a quiet. Not pain. Not absence. More like something that was always moving — you never noticed it until it stopped."

Which brings the story to now. The harvest is so efficient it is invisible. The most productive collection node in the city presents as a transit hub. And the thing under the Meridian is almost through — two centuries of accumulated output have very nearly finished what it could never finish alone.

The city has spent its entire existence working a splint loose.

A few years ago, the Bureau sent a team to silence a Summoner named Vesper. An enforcer named Reid let her walk, put down the colleague who tried to stop him, and filed a false report. He was stripped for it. He would do it again — and the certainty, not the regret, is his problem.

Now she's gone. Her father asks him to find her. He takes the case because it's Crane asking, and because some things you don't leave alone.

CHAPTER IX

The Doors Are Wounds

The two centuries are not joined by a machine or a place. They are joined by injuries.

When the newly chartered office first ran extraction at industrial scale, it cut deep enough to leave holes in the thing it was drawing from — and two centuries of continuous harvest have kept those holes from closing. A scar you keep drawing from is a scar that never heals.

Those holes are the doors. To cross between centuries is to walk through the wound, which means walking inside the thing itself. And because a wound only connects to the moment it was made, there is exactly one past era to reach — the founding. The doors lead back to the crime because the crime made them.

The founding crime cut the only holes
through which the founding crime can be witnessed. The crime is the evidence of itself.

Not everyone can use them. Metered output is a tether: if your power runs through the collection grid, you are anchored to the century the grid is collecting in. The harvest doesn't only take your power — it takes your ability to leave.

Which means everyone who can move through time is someone the system failed to capture. Reid crosses because the system deemed him beneath it: no Grade, no Academy, nothing it ever wanted. His disgrace, reframed as the thing that saves him.

And the past cannot be changed. The charter was signed, the coat was taken, the quarter burned. What the party can do is witness, document, and come back carrying evidence that changes what the present means. The past is a crime you can only read, never prevent.

One more rule, which is a lie: the party is told early that scar tissue is numb and the thing cannot feel them crossing. Later, a door they have used before is attended when they arrive. Nothing is being counted. It notices when it notices.

Canon status what is settled, what is draft

Established canon: the city, the Bureau, the Grade system, calibration, the gold coat, WARDEN, the entity's two registers, the present-day cast and Episode One's cases.

Draft — designed, not yet propagated: everything about the revolution era and the prehistory. The Patrimony, the Deep Patron, the Accountants, the child tithe, the Night of the Lamps, Varenne, Thibault, Sabine, the Registrar, and the entity's stranded state. Working names throughout.

Known conflict: existing lore says the entity "wanted to be built around." A stranded, inert thing cannot want anything — the fact survives, the intention does not, and that line needs rewording before any of this becomes canon.

Open: who speaks the prehistory aloud and where; what stands on the quarantined quarter today; whether the Night of the Lamps is played or witnessed; the survivor's name; and the episode map.

Reid

Fighter · Protagonist
Bureau status
Disgraced — stripped
Grade
None. Non-practitioner.
Crosses time
Yes — nothing ever wanted anything from him

Born, trained and disgraced in the Heights. He knows which noodle stand opens at 3am and which clerk takes bribes in the form of good whisky. He was never a practitioner — no Grade, no sigil, no Academy — and spent twenty years working beside people who were, which means he knows exactly what the system looks like from outside it.

The night the Bureau sent a team to disappear Vesper, Reid was on it as borrowed muscle. He let her walk. When a colleague moved to stop him, he put the man down and left a scar through his left brow, then filed a false report that couldn't be challenged without someone admitting they'd been physically obstructed. The Bureau took the report at face value and stripped him. Cleaner that way.

His wound: he did the right thing, it cost him everything, and he'd do it again. The certainty is the problem, not the regret. What he won't examine is whether the life he built afterward is actually living.

She moves jailbroken sigils — Bureau hardware with the return channel cut — to people who need to stop being metered. Off-grid from birth: no license, no file, no record. The Bureau has nothing on her because it has nothing of her, and she has been operating at full output her whole life without knowing the gap exists.

She is genuinely funny, likes loud rooms, and runs every morning — rain, no rain, 3am after a job. It isn't fitness. She learned young that stopping is how you get caught.

Eighteen months ago she stepped into a fight to help a fourteen-year-old called Lain and then couldn't walk away. Lain is her at that age, same city, same margins. Staying for Lain means looking at herself, which she doesn't do.

Her wound: she has run so many covers for so long that she doesn't know what's underneath. The question exists. She doesn't ask it.

Sprawl-born, Sprawl-stayed. For decades he has treated megacorp workers with industrial injuries, people who can't afford licensed care, and practitioners who were calibrated and lost more than their output. The Bureau tolerates him because the Sprawl needs healers and he has never been worth the paperwork.

He treated Vesper for years — first ordinary injuries, then presentations he had no category for, getting worse each visit. She gave him pieces and he didn't push. Then she stopped coming, and he filed it under not my business.

His wound: he has been the cleanup crew for a machine he couldn't name, patching the aftermath and never touching the cause. He processes out loud, at length, and argues with things until they make sense. What he won't examine is whether staying in his lane was wisdom or avoidance.
